02 / Mobile game UX

Livin’ America

Where first-time players lose their bearings

I audited the first session of a mobile life simulation and designed a clearer way for players to choose a trait.

The challenge

Where do first-time players lose their bearings?

Livin’ America asks players to make life choices from their first session. I looked at whether a new player could tell what each screen was asking, read at their own pace, and understand who they were playing.

Role
Usability audit and UI concept
Methods
80-minute walkthrough and heuristic review, with App Store reviews as context
Status
Proposed trait-selection concept

Three onboarding findings

Original screens and proposed design

Finding 01

Make the selection rule explicit

Walkthrough observation. The trait screen left me wondering whether I could pick one trait or several.

Recommendation. If the step allows one trait, say “Choose one trait” above the options and give the selected card a clear radio-style indicator.

Proposed interface · Finding 01

One choice, two states

The instruction sets the rule up front. The selected card confirms the choice through its border, radio indicator, and label. Continue becomes available after a trait is selected.

Finding 02

Let players read at their own pace

Walkthrough observation. Automatic scrolling made the customs instructions hard to finish, and an overlay interrupted reading.

Recommendation. Use manual scrolling and keep the instructions accessible until the player is ready to continue.

Finding 03

Explain whose story is starting

Walkthrough observation. The story treated the player as 18 after an older age was entered during setup.

Recommendation. If 18 is a fixed starting age, explain the premise before the first story choice. If setup is meant to personalize the character, carry that choice into the story.

Audit details

Walkthrough and sources

Walkthrough. I spent 80 minutes tracing the first-session flow, capturing screens and evaluating where the interface made a choice or its consequences hard to understand. The three findings above come from that walkthrough.

Secondary context. I also consulted iOS App Store reviews. I’d use interviews or onboarding tests to explore whether the same issues recur with new players.

Illustrative player profile

Jo · Career transition

Jo represents a possible player who wants to explore a life change after gaining work experience. I’d use this profile to frame a research question about how older players interpret the game’s opening premise.

View the complete audit · 17 steps

1. Legal Disclaimer Screen

"The disclaimer needs to be simpler to understand." Dense legal copy at initial launch creates onboarding friction.

Show main legal points in short bullet points, with a link to read full terms if needed.

2. Login Options & Button Layout

"The lower button is cut off. Add username/password option." Layout elements clipping on certain screen dimensions affect visual polish.

Fix bottom spacing so buttons aren't cut off, and support standard login input fields.

3. Google Login Transition Animation

"The photo animation moved in a funky way and was slow." Transition timing following authentication shapes performance impression.

Speed up loading animations and make them fade in smoothly without progress freezes.

4. Graduation Prompt Framing

"Who are you... truly? Direction needed." Structural indicators orient players when encountering reflective story choices.

Add a clear label like [Scenario Question] above the prompt and frame choices in first-person statements.

5. Trait Selection Guidance & Mechanics

"Can I select more than one trait?" Clear visual UI affordances establish single vs. multi-select expectations.

Use standard radio buttons and add a short helper note explaining to pick only one trait.

6. Microcopy & Global Accessibility ('Hazard Pay')

"Love the humor! But non-native speakers might miss 'hazard pay'." Witty personality enriches brand while accessibility ensures global comprehension.

Maintain the fun tone while ensuring core game rules are explained clearly for all audiences.

7. Family Wealth Text Logic

"Sentences contradict with 'But' when they shouldn't." Conjunctions work best when highlighting explicit contrast.

Rewrite text logic: "Everyone's story starts at the beginning... But not everyone starts with equal resources."

8. Spinning Wheel Progress & Context

"Waited 30 seconds and screen got stuck on 'Not bad...'." Progress freezes hurt trust; context prior to spinning sets expectation.

Fix progress freeze bug and add context explaining why checking family wealth matters.

9. Game Start Hook & Session Saving

"Interactive phone call was exciting! What about saving my stage?" Session persistence ensures returning users resume progress seamlessly.

Keep interactive phone call for new users, but auto-resume progress for returning accounts.

10. Player Age Consistency

"App said I'm 18 even though I registered an older age." Dynamic story copy matching user input maintains narrative immersion.

Dynamically inject the exact age entered during registration into narrative story text.

11. City Selection Narrative & Airline Tie-in

"Why is Chicago a great selection? Connect to American Airlines better." Atmospheric city details make partner integrations feel organic.

Add city highlight details before seamlessly introducing the airline sponsor integration.

12. Google Maps Zoom Sequence

"Map starts already focused on Chicago." Starting zoomed out establishes visual storytelling of journey travel.

Start camera zoomed out globally, then smoothly animate moving across continents to Chicago.

13. Customs Screen Pace & Touch Controls

"Fast automatic text scroll made rules unreadable." Touch interruptions degrade reading control during informational steps.

Allow manual drag-scrolling at player speed and remove interrupting warning overlays.

14. Apartment Options & Typography

"Take it or keep looking wasn't explicit. Font sizes differed." Explicit trade-off labels and uniform font sizing reinforce clarity.

Standardize button typography and label options clearly: 'Accept Free Apartment' vs 'Explore Upgrade'.

15. Creative Career Paths & Information Cards

"I chose to work in Design, and there were only 2 options: architect and interior designer. I felt like I would like a representation of graphic designers. I thought that the information behind the cards was very helpful and detailed enough. I was able to make a sensible decision."

The flip-card design pattern effectively informs player choices; expanding career options enhances creative representation.

Keep flip card design, but add more creative job paths like Graphic Designer and UX/UI Designer.

16. Job Market Realism & Interview Concept

"Job pool feels realistic. Would love an interview demonstration." Interactive hiring simulations build authentic engagement.

Add recruitment mini-games and simulated recruiter interviews for practical life-skill practice.

17. Demographic Representation Across Life Stages

"Relocating to the USA happens across all age groups and experience levels." Multiple life-stage branches expand player market reach.

Offer optional setup tracks (Student, Young Professional, Career Relocator) during onboarding.

Reflection

Test the premise before expanding the story

Clarifying that the story starts at 18 is a small copy change. Supporting multiple life stages would also change the scenarios, choices, and progression.

I’d test the clearer setup with first-time players across age groups and ask them who they’re playing and how they know. If players understood the premise and still wanted to explore later-life scenarios, I’d prototype one alternative opening before designing more story tracks.
